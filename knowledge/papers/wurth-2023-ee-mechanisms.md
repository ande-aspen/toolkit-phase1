## Resumen
Systematic review of 136 empirical papers on causal mechanisms in entrepreneurial ecosystems. Organizes mechanisms into four categories: interdependencies within ecosystems, upward causation (ecosystem to entrepreneurial outputs), downward causation (outputs back to ecosystem), and socioeconomic outcomes. Provides empirical evidence on element clusters, knowledge spillovers, network effects, resource munificence, legitimacy/culture, entrepreneurial recycling, and path dependence. Validates the multiplicative composite index approach for ecosystem measurement, showing that the weakest element has disproportionate negative impact.

## Insights Clave

### INS-1: Four categories of ecosystem mechanisms
> The review organizes mechanisms into: (1) interdependencies within the ecosystem — how elements relate and reinforce each other; (2) upward causation — how ecosystem context produces entrepreneurship; (3) ecosystem to socioeconomic outcomes — how entrepreneurial output translates into value; (4) downward causation and path dependence — how past entrepreneurial activity feeds back into the ecosystem. ANDE Condition domains (P, S, H, I) operate in categories 1-2; Result domains (F, C, M) capture category 3; category 4 explains when Conditions are strong but Results weak ("connection failure") or Results strong but Conditions weak ("fragile system").

**Ubicación:** Extracto 6A (Sections 3-5)
**Relevancia:** Provides theoretical grounding for ANDE diagnostic logic. The four-category framework explains WHY certain domain combinations produce specific outcomes, enabling more nuanced analysis in Section 5 of reports.

### INS-2: Three interdependency clusters among ecosystem elements
> Empirical analysis reveals three strongly interdependent clusters: (1) Talent-Culture-Services — education levels, entrepreneurial culture, and business services form a self-reinforcing subsystem; (2) Knowledge-Leadership — R&D investment and innovation project leaders are strongly correlated, reflecting the knowledge economy dimension; (3) Infrastructure-Demand — physical infrastructure and market demand are strongly correlated, reflecting population/prosperity. Counter-intuitive finding: governance quality is consistently negatively correlated with other elements in Dutch regions, suggesting formal institutional quality may not drive other elements in advanced economies.

**Ubicación:** Extracto 6B (Section 5.1)
**Relevancia:** When scoring interdependent indicators, expect correlated patterns. If S1-S5 score high but H1-H5 score low, there may be a measurement problem or unusual decoupling worth investigating. The three clusters provide a template for identifying whether ecosystem strengths are concentrated in one subsystem or distributed.

### INS-3: Four upward causation mechanisms — from ecosystem to entrepreneurship
> (1) Knowledge spillovers: universities generate knowledge that entrepreneurs commercialize; geographically bounded and diminishing with distance. (2) Network effects: social networks distribute knowledge, labor, and capital; the "dealmaker" role (Feldman & Zoller 2012) is key — a small number of highly connected individuals bridging ecosystem parts; network structure matters more than size. (3) Resource munificence: availability of VC, talent, and support services reduces entry/growth barriers, but resources must be accessible (not just available), which depends on intermediary organizations. (4) Legitimacy and culture: social norms, failure tolerance, and visible role models create a supportive context; hardest to measure but strongly supported empirically.

**Ubicación:** Extracto 6C (Section 5.2)
**Relevancia:** Explains WHY specific indicators matter. When scoring F3 (VCs) or F4 (angels), assess accessibility not just count — a VC based locally but investing elsewhere contributes less than the number suggests. For S4 (mentors), network quality ("dealmakers") may matter more than quantity.

### INS-4: Entrepreneurial recycling — the downward causation feedback loop
> Empirical evidence shows founders of high-growth firms subsequently become VCs, angel investors, mentors, board members, and ecosystem organization leaders. Prevalence of high-growth firms at time T positively correlates with subsequent ecosystem element values at T+3, particularly market demand, project leadership, business services, and venture capital availability. Very strong intertemporal correlations in element values suggest high path dependence: strong regions stay strong, weak regions stay weak. Early intervention matters disproportionately.

**Ubicación:** Extracto 6D (Section 5.4)
**Relevancia:** For nascent/emerging ecosystems, the absence of exits (F5) and active angels (F4) is both symptom and cause. The recycling mechanism is absent. Recommendations should target creating the first generation of recyclers through diaspora entrepreneurs, corporate spin-offs, or importing experienced mentors from mature ecosystems.

### INS-5: Multiplicative index validates bottleneck logic
> Multivariate regression of individual elements on entrepreneurial output is inappropriate (substantively: no one-to-one relationships in ecosystems; statistically: high multicollinearity). Three index types tested on Dutch data: additive (E1+E2+...+En), multiplicative (E1*E2*...*En), and log-multiplicative. The multiplicative index best captures the interactive nature — a weakness in any single element drags the entire index disproportionately. This provides the mathematical basis for the "bottleneck" concept. R-squared of the ecosystem index predicting high-growth firms: 0.437 (2009), 0.625 (2012), 0.526 (2015).

**Ubicación:** Extracto 6E (Section 6.2)
**Relevancia:** Validates ANDE's approach of identifying bottlenecks. A multiplicative model means policy priority should be raising the floor (addressing the weakest domain) rather than raising the ceiling (strengthening already-strong domains). Directly supports flagging the lowest-scoring domain as the critical bottleneck.

### INS-6: Inter-ecosystem linkages — ecosystems are not closed systems
> Three inter-ecosystem mechanisms: talent mobility (entrepreneurs/workers move between regions carrying knowledge and networks), investment flows (VC is increasingly less locally bounded — external investors can strengthen or weaken local ecosystems), knowledge flows (through universities, conferences, professional networks). Some elements are more locally bounded than others: culture and networks are strongly local; finance and knowledge are increasingly global; policy operates at multiple levels. An ecosystem dependent entirely on external capital is vulnerable to investment cycles.

**Ubicación:** Extracto 6F (Section 3.5)
**Relevancia:** When evaluating F1 (total investment) or F3 (local VCs), consider whether capital is endogenous or exogenous. A city that produces talent but loses it to larger hubs has a "leak" undermining other investments. For H4 (tech talent), retention matters as much as production.

### INS-7: Five policy implications from empirical evidence
> (1) System-level intervention, not element-level — Scotland's Intermediate Technology Initiative failed by investing heavily in knowledge creation without complementary network and financing infrastructure. (2) Sequencing matters — culture and human capital provide the base; services and networks enable activity; financing and markets follow demonstrated activity. (3) Do not "pick winners" — bottom-up enabling conditions outperform government selection of sectors/firms. (4) Systemic measurement — individual indicators miss interactive effects; composite indices are more predictive. (5) Long time horizons — feedback loops operate on 3-10 year cycles; premature policy abandonment is a common failure mode.

**Ubicación:** Extracto 6G (Section 7)
**Relevancia:** Reinforces ANDE's sequential approach (Table 4.3): build Culture and Human Capital first, then Services, then Policy and Financing, then Markets and R&D. Scotland's failure case is a cautionary example for any ecosystem investing in I-domain without S-domain and F-domain support. Recommendations must include realistic time horizons.
