## Resumen
Practitioner-oriented guide (124 pages) for development professionals who have completed ecosystem mapping, covering intervention design at three levels: culture, interacting players, and business environment. Supported by 12+ GIZ project case studies from developing economies (Sudan, Colombia, Namibia, Egypt, Rwanda, India, Vietnam, Jordan, Sri Lanka, Mexico, Senegal, Ghana) and 13 non-GIZ programme interviews. Provides actionable frameworks for ESO design, financial ecosystem development, culture interventions, gender inclusion, mentoring programme design, regulatory reform, collaboration structures, and programme design principles.

## Insights Clave

### INS-1: Three-Level Ecosystem Framework
> GIZ organizes ecosystems into three nested levels: (1) Entrepreneurial Culture -- prevailing norms that reward or disincentivize innovation and risk-taking; (2) Interacting Players -- entrepreneurs, ESOs, financial players, universities, government agencies, mentors; (3) Business Environment -- policies, regulations, and institutional environment. Culture underpins everything; players must exist before policies can be optimized.

**Ubicación:** Extracto 27A
**Relevancia:** Maps to ANDE domains: Level 1 = Culture (C), Level 2 = Support Services (S), Finance (F), Human Capital (H), Markets (M), Level 3 = Policy (P), partially Innovation (I). Implies a sequencing for interventions based on which level scores lowest.

### INS-2: Ecosystem Strengthening vs. Direct Entrepreneur Support
> Ecosystem strengthening (supporting local ESOs, building multiplier effects) creates long-term sustainable impact with higher local ownership but is harder to measure. Direct entrepreneur support produces faster, measurable impacts but has limited reach. Nascent ecosystems (ANDE 0-25) may need direct support first to create demonstration effects; Emergent and above should prioritize ecosystem-level interventions.

**Ubicación:** Extracto 27C
**Relevancia:** Critical for calibrating Section 7 recommendations to ecosystem maturity level. Avoids the common error of recommending ecosystem-level interventions in places where no demonstration effects yet exist.

### INS-3: ESO Types, Characteristics, and Quality Findings
> Five ESO types (accelerator, incubator, competition, training program, mentoring/peer network) serve different segments with different models. Key GALI/AfriLabs findings: many ESO professionals lack entrepreneurial experience and should facilitate experts rather than train directly; generalized workshops have little impact and sometimes distract from real business challenges; few programs focus on later-stage/growth companies, inhibiting scaling.

**Ubicación:** Extracto 27D
**Relevancia:** When scoring S1-S2, differentiate between active programs with documented cohorts vs. organizations that exist in name only. Supports recommending quality improvements for existing ESOs, not just counting them.

### INS-4: ESO Financial Sustainability -- Four Revenue Sources
> ESOs fund operations through: (1) service income (space rental, training fees), (2) investment in success (equity, royalties), (3) grants and donations, (4) commercial activities (consulting, technology commercialization). Most need multiple sources; single-source dependence creates fragility. Risk mitigation for donor-funded ESOs includes challenge grants, matching funding requirements, and specified exit plans.

**Ubicación:** Extracto 27E
**Relevancia:** When assessing Support Services quality, investigate whether local ESOs have diversified income. Ecosystems where ESOs depend entirely on donor or government grants are structurally fragile.

### INS-5: Financial Ecosystem Gap and Three-Pillar Intervention
> A USD 3.3 trillion financing gap exists for entrepreneurs in emerging markets with an early-stage capital gap between EUR 15,000 and EUR 2M. Three intervention pillars: (1) increase supply of finance through capacity building and matching capital, (2) matchmaking through events, platforms, and brokers, (3) improve entrepreneur readiness through investment readiness training and part-time CFO services. All three must be addressed simultaneously.

**Ubicación:** Extracto 27F
**Relevancia:** When Finance is the bottleneck domain, merely counting VCs (F3) or angels (F4) is insufficient. The readiness of entrepreneurs to absorb capital and matchmaking infrastructure both matter. For Nascent ecosystems, culture and ESOs should come before finance interventions.

### INS-6: Culture Interventions -- Sequenced Approach
> Cultural alignment toolkit: storytelling (building acceptance), pilot programming (small cohort with narrow objectives, must succeed), external stakeholder facilitation (alignment), and communicating success (media channels). The Isenberg/Babson Scale-Up model (Manizales, Colombia) demonstrates the sequence: convince one funder, run small cohort, develop success stories, communicate through local media, create buzz and trust, attract more stakeholders, establish local institution, scale.

**Ubicación:** Extracto 27H
**Relevancia:** When Culture scores low, recommendations should follow this sequence rather than jumping to large culture programmes. The Manizales Scale-Up model is directly applicable to Latin American ecosystems.

### INS-7: Mentoring Design Principles and Impact Evidence
> RCT in Togo: traditional business training showed NO income increase; coaching/mentoring group showed 40% increase in profits. Four mentor types (business mentor, adviser, coach, peer supporter) serve different needs. Key design principles: recruit mentors who have walked the same path, provide orientation for both parties, match by personality (not just industry), facilitate through structured goal-setting sessions. Industry-based matching can create mistrust from perceived competition.

**Ubicación:** Extracto 27J
**Relevancia:** When scoring S4 (Mentores), existence of structured programmes with proper matching and facilitation matters more than raw numbers. Recommendations should include recruitment, training, and structured design, not just "increase mentors."

### INS-8: Startup Acts -- Legislative Innovation
> Startup Acts define "startup" legally, establish preferential tax/regulatory treatment, reform company entry/exit, coordinate with economic strategy, and provide specific support mechanisms. Existing examples: Italy (2012, first), Argentina, India, Tunisia, Senegal, Mali. Tax incentives are less controversial than direct financial support and create less market distortion. The intangible signal that government recognizes entrepreneurship is especially valuable where broad reform proves difficult.

**Ubicación:** Extracto 27L
**Relevancia:** Relevant for P2 (Regimen fiscal startups) and P3 (Programas publicos). If an ecosystem lacks a Startup Act or equivalent, this is a concrete recommendation. Tunisia and Senegal cases are relevant for Latin American ecosystems at similar maturity levels.
