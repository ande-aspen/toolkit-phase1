## Resumen
Edited volume of 20 chapters spanning women entrepreneurship, AI in entrepreneurship, behavioral/knowledge management, and sustainability. Five priority chapters extracted here cover: an outcome-based Innovation Density Funnel model mapping 7 actor types across 5 stages (Ch. 5); qualitative data on AI adoption barriers among Mexican startup founders (Ch. 11); Gen Z social entrepreneurship intentions from 300 Mexico City students (Ch. 13); the dual mediator/moderator role of opportunity recognition between social networks and knowledge acquisition (Ch. 16); and the Entrepreneurship 5.0 framework integrating human-centric technology with sustainability (Ch. 20).

## Insights Clave

### INS-1: Innovation Density Funnel — actor-stage matrix for ecosystem diagnosis
> The framework organizes the ecosystem as a 7-actor x 5-stage matrix (Inspired People through Scale-ups), delineating specific responsibilities at each intersection. A "Backbone Organization" coordinates all actors toward collective impact, preventing fragmentation. The five-dimension application process: establish ideal state, develop metrics, map current state, conduct gap analysis, prioritize actions.

**Ubicación:** Chapter 5, pp. 145-177 (Extractos 5A, 5D)
**Relevancia:** Complementary diagnostic model to ANDE. The actor-stage matrix offers a more granular view of who should do what at each funnel stage, useful for Section 5 (strategic analysis) and Section 7 (recommendations) of ANDE reports.

### INS-2: Measurable indicators per actor and funnel level
> Table 5.2 provides specific measurable indicators for each actor at each funnel stage. Examples: % of students attending 8+ entrepreneurship experiences (Academy, Level 1); # of deals and $ invested in pre-seed/seed projects (Capital, Level 2); # of technology startups with 1+ year operation originating from local universities (Academy, Level 4); % of tech-based scale-ups from incubation programs (Service Providers, Level 5).

**Ubicación:** Chapter 5, Extracto 5B
**Relevancia:** Provides concrete metrics that complement ANDE's 30 indicators, especially for benchmarking academia and service provider contributions at each stage.

### INS-3: AI adoption barriers among Mexican startup founders
> From 16 in-depth interviews in Mexico City: 68.75% suffer low-trust issues with AI due to cost-time-risk factors; 87.5% feel "less known" to AI information causing cognitive exhaustion; 56.25% report cognitive exhaustion from AI information chaos. Only 43.75% are comfortable exploring AI as value addition. Key recommendation: nodal entrepreneurs confident in AI should serve as peer training instructors.

**Ubicación:** Chapter 11, pp. 289-316 (Extractos 11A, 11E)
**Relevancia:** Provides Mexico-specific data on AI adoption barriers for ecosystem diagnostics. Relevant for technology readiness assessments and recommendations on AI integration in H-domain and I-domain.

### INS-4: Gen Z entrepreneurial vs. social entrepreneurial intention gap
> Among 300 university students in Mexico City: general entrepreneurial intention averages 3.92/5, but social entrepreneurial intention drops to 3.30/5. None identified as current social entrepreneurs. 81.6% agreed their dream job should have positive social/environmental impact, yet AI showed no significant correlation with social entrepreneurial intention (r=0.041). Over 98% of Mexican organizations are MSMEs (~4.7M), employing 68%+ of workforce; 47.3% led by women.

**Ubicación:** Chapter 13, pp. 335-353 (Extractos 13A, 13B, 13C)
**Relevancia:** Quantifies the intention-action gap among next-gen entrepreneurs in Mexico. The high dream-job-impact aspiration (81.6%) vs. low social entrepreneurship action signals a policy opportunity. Mexico MSME baseline data useful for context in diagnostics.

### INS-5: Opportunity recognition as dual mediator and moderator
> SEM analysis (n=169, Monterrey) shows opportunity recognition simultaneously mediates (translates social connections into knowledge: SN->OR->Knowledge) and moderates (amplifies the SN-Knowledge relationship when OR is high). Even bonding social capital (family, close friends) can generate significant knowledge acquisition when opportunity recognition skills are present — challenging the notion that bonding networks are inherently less valuable.

**Ubicación:** Chapter 16, pp. 415-441 (Extractos 16A, 16B, 16C)
**Relevancia:** Directly applicable to C-domain and S-domain recommendations. Mentor programs should include opportunity recognition training alongside traditional mentoring. Particularly relevant for LatAm ecosystems where family/community ties are culturally strong.

### INS-6: Three types of social capital and knowledge typology
> Bonding (family/friends, emotional support), bridging (cross-group, diverse information), and linking (power/authority figures, strategic support) social capital each serve different entrepreneurial functions. Tacit knowledge (experiential, intuitive) and explicit knowledge (codified, transferable) are both enhanced through networks but require opportunity recognition as the activation mechanism.

**Ubicación:** Chapter 16, Extractos 16D, 16E
**Relevancia:** Framework for designing ecosystem interventions that build all three types of social capital. Linking social capital (mentors, industry leaders) is essential for navigating formal structures — directly relevant to S4 (mentors) scoring.

### INS-7: Entrepreneurship 5.0 and green entrepreneurship in developing economies
> Entrepreneurship 5.0 integrates human creativity with AI/robotics for sustainable solutions, emphasizing human centricity and ESG evaluation of tech-based MSMEs. Green entrepreneurship through generative AI is nascent — mechanisms are unclear in developing countries where government support remains obscure. Consortium strategies linking local SMEs with MNCs through Industry 5.0 partnerships offer a practical model for market depth in emerging ecosystems.

**Ubicación:** Chapter 20, pp. 535-557 (Extractos 20A, 20B, 20C, 20E)
**Relevancia:** Provides forward-looking framework for I-domain and M-domain recommendations, particularly for ecosystems seeking to integrate sustainability and technology. ESG as an ecosystem maturity indicator is a useful addition to diagnostic assessments.
