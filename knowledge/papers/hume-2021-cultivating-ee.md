## Resumen
ANDE evidence synthesis summarizing existing research on cultivating entrepreneurial ecosystems, designed as a resource for practitioners working with small and growing businesses (SGBs: 5-250 employees, $20K-$2M growth capital). Identifies three core lessons, three ecosystem maturity stages, five critical development factors, and provides six case studies (NYC, Caribbean EPIC, Start-Up Chile, Estonia, Kampala, Kuala Lumpur). Key finding: mature ecosystems share common characteristics, but growth paths are non-linear and interventions must build on local strengths.

## Insights Clave

### INS-1: Three core lessons for ecosystem building
> (1) Mature ecosystems share common characteristics: strong networks, entrepreneurial culture, support organizations, finance providers, and supportive policy. (2) Ecosystem development involves complex, non-linear interactions — no singular growth path; communities must build on existing strengths. (3) Interventions should build upon local attributes; policymakers should focus on creating a strong foundation so the ecosystem can respond to opportunities as they arise organically. SMEs contribute up to 40% of GDP in developing economies and 90% of jobs worldwide.

**Ubicación:** Extracto 5A (pp. 5-6)
**Relevancia:** Foundational framing for any ANDE diagnostic. Every recommendation must respect non-linear, context-specific growth. These three principles should guide Section 7 (Recommendations) of every report.

### INS-2: Three stages of ecosystem maturity
> Activation (nascent): <1,000 startups, limited founder experience, significant resource gaps, few financing opportunities (e.g., Kampala). Growth (evolving): elements become specialized, high-profile entrepreneurs with some exits, investors more open to risk (e.g., Kuala Lumpur, $321M raised in 2019). Mature: globally recognized unicorns, exits >$1B, effective policies, global connectedness (e.g., Singapore with $7.4B early-stage market, Silicon Valley). Growth path between stages is non-linear; most mature ecosystems developed organically.

**Ubicación:** Extracto 5B (pp. 7-8)
**Relevancia:** Maps directly to ANDE maturity levels (Naciente, Emergente, En Desarrollo, Autosostenible). Use these stage descriptions and examples to contextualize maturity scores and set appropriate expectations.

### INS-3: Five critical factors for ecosystem development
> (1) Strong networks among entrepreneurs and institutions — NYC Angels, Lagos Angels Network, Atlanta counter-example (strong factors but social fragmentation = stagnation). (2) Entrepreneurial culture — tolerance of failure, "pay it forward" behavior, serial entrepreneurs. (3) Anchor institutions — universities, large corporations, R&D labs; startup rates higher near research universities. (4) Early-stage finance — mature ecosystems have "strong, dense, accessible" VC/angel communities; government can fill gaps in nascent ecosystems. (5) Strong policy environment — decriminalizing bankruptcy, simplifying business creation, infrastructure investment.

**Ubicación:** Extracto 5C (pp. 9-13)
**Relevancia:** Maps directly to ANDE's 7 domains. The Atlanta counter-example is critical evidence: strong individual factors without network connectivity = stagnation. The Lagos Angels Network shows angel network creation is achievable through donor intervention. Connectivity between elements matters as much as individual domain scores.

### INS-4: Build on local strengths, entrepreneurs must lead
> Policymakers should use local conditions as building blocks, not emulate other ecosystems. Oxford leveraged Heathrow proximity; Swiss medtech grew from watch-making precision; serendipity plays a role (2008 crisis spurred NYC tech). Taiwan retained 40,000 US-trained engineers (1988-1998) to build IT sector. Critical finding: Bangalore outperformed Nairobi because it was driven by successful entrepreneurs rather than NGO initiatives. "State interventions without sufficiently thick networks based on a supportive culture will likely have limited impact" (Spigel & Harrison 2017).

**Ubicación:** Extracto 5D (pp. 13-14)
**Relevancia:** Recommendations must identify which local actors can lead implementation. If C-domain (culture/networks) is weak, top-down interventions will have limited impact. The Bangalore vs. Nairobi comparison provides evidence for entrepreneur-led development over NGO/donor-driven models.

### INS-5: NYC case study — diagnostic-first intervention model
> Post-2008 crisis: NYC lost 90,000 jobs, consumer spending fell 11%. NYCEDC + mayor's office conducted stakeholder-driven tech startup analysis, identified 4 challenges, designed 4 targeted policies: co-working spaces (physical space gap), university attraction via PPP (talent gap), city-supported seed fund (capital gap), competitions + marketing (community gap). Results by 2015: 2nd largest US tech ecosystem, >14,000 new ventures, $7B VC investment, high-tech sector = 12% of tax revenue, 18% annual job growth.

**Ubicación:** Extracto 5E (pp. 15-16)
**Relevancia:** Template for structuring ANDE report recommendations: diagnostic first, then targeted policies per gap. The 4-challenge/4-response matrix is directly applicable to Section 7. Demonstrates that city-level interventions combining talent, capital, and community can trigger rapid growth.

### INS-6: Start-Up Chile — talent attraction with retention challenge
> Chile's entrepreneurial activity grew from 17% to 26% (2020), peaking at 37% (2019). Start-Up Chile: equity-free $40,000 + 4-month incubation + "social capital points" requiring community reinvestment. 1,500+ entrepreneurs from 80+ countries, >$1B raised by participants. But retention problem: underdeveloped local VC market, investors prefer local companies, require large equity stakes. Most graduates seek investment outside Chile and relocate. SCALE program ($100,000 for growth-stage willing to incorporate in Chile) attempted to address this.

**Ubicación:** Extracto 5G (p. 18)
**Relevancia:** Benchmark for P1 (1-day incorporation), P2 (equity-free seed capital), P3 (public programs). Demonstrates that strong P+S interventions can build culture through social capital requirements, but F-domain weakness creates retention risk. Relevant for any ecosystem where policy support outpaces financing maturity.

### INS-7: Accelerators create entrepreneurial clusters and attract investors
> Graduating startups more likely to attract equity financing even after controlling for selection effects (Lall et al. 2020). Study of 59 accelerators in 38 US metros: establishing an accelerator increases early-stage investors and can "create an entrepreneurial cluster where it did not exist before" (Fehder & Hochberg 2014). USAID PACE program (est. 2013): multi-faceted approach combining direct investment, de-risking via guarantees, and capacity development.

**Ubicación:** Extracto 5J (pp. 11-12)
**Relevancia:** Evidence that S1 (accelerator presence) directly drives F-domain outcomes. Accelerators generate spillover investment activity — consider not just count but capacity for cluster creation when scoring S1.

### INS-8: Government-backed finance benchmarks and fertile soil principle
> USAID MENA II (Lebanon): 125+ angels trained, $12.7M investments, 50% equity guarantee. Ghana VCTF: leveraged seed into >$50M private funding, created angel network (2011) and SME stock exchange listing. Public VC funds less effective in developing/corrupt economies but improve when syndicated with private investors. Concluding principle: focus on creating "fertile soil" rather than directing outcomes, because a significant degree of randomness and serendipity is involved in ecosystem development.

**Ubicación:** Extractos 5K (p. 11), 5I (p. 20)
**Relevancia:** Ghana VCTF model (seed fund -> angel network -> SME exchange) represents a complete F-domain development pathway. The "fertile soil" metaphor supports prioritizing Condition domains (P, S, H, I) before expecting Outcome domains (F, C, M) to flourish.
