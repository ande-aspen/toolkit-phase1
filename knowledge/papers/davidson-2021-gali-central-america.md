## Resumen
GALI Knowledge Brief analyzing 809 ventures that applied to 21 acceleration programs in Central America (2013-2018), operated by 7 organizations. Examines pre-acceleration financing baselines, fundraising expectations vs. actuals, desired benefits of acceleration, gender gaps, accelerator-investor relationship models, and structural barriers to financing in small/volatile economies. The core finding is a 97% equity fundraising shortfall for accelerated ventures.

## Insights Clave

### INS-1: Only 40% of accelerator applicants have any prior financing
> Of 809 Central American ventures applying to accelerators: 73% were post-revenue, 79% had at least 1 full-time employee, but only 40% had received ANY financing. Most common source was debt (banks dominate). Funded ventures were older (7.4 vs 6.9 years) and 2x more likely to earn revenue. Energy sector most likely to have funding (70%); infrastructure least (19%). Acceptance rates were nearly identical for funded vs. unfunded ventures (41% vs 39%).

**Ubicación:** Extracto 4A (pp. 7-8)
**Relevancia:** Baseline benchmark for F4 (angel activity), F6 (seed capital), and S1 (accelerator effectiveness) in nascent LatAm ecosystems. The 40% figure quantifies the pre-acceleration financing gap in Central America.

### INS-2: 97% equity fundraising shortfall — the critical gap
> Among 255 accelerated ventures tracked post-program: 33% expected to raise equity, but only 5% actually did. Average expected equity: $103,707; average raised: $2,740 — a 97% shortfall. Debt performed better (38% expected, 26% achieved). Central American entrepreneurs seek more debt and less equity vs. broader LatAm (32% vs 47% seeking equity). Entrepreneurs often not prepared to receive equity: cannot demonstrate scalability, resist outside ownership, are averse to foreign investors.

**Ubicación:** Extracto 4B (pp. 9-12)
**Relevancia:** Fundamental benchmark for F1-F4 scoring. The 97% equity gap quantifies the most severe financing bottleneck. Reveals that the gap is both supply-side (no investors) and demand-side (entrepreneurs unprepared for equity). Critical for diagnosing whether accelerator-investor linkages are functional.

### INS-3: Entrepreneurs prioritize skills and networks over investor access
> Top desired benefits of acceleration: business skills (26%), network development (20%), direct funding (19%), mentorship (14%), access to investors (10%). "Access to investors" ranks only 5th. Entrepreneurs prefer accelerator-provided capital over investor matchmaking. Direct funding from the accelerator outranks investor connections.

**Ubicación:** Extracto 4C (p. 10)
**Relevancia:** Challenges the assumption that accelerators should focus on investor matchmaking. When evaluating S1 (accelerator quality), assess delivery of skills, networks, and direct capital — not just demo days. Programs that only offer investor access miss primary entrepreneur demand.

### INS-4: 13-point gender gap in pre-acceleration financing
> Pre-acceleration investment by team composition: women-only teams 27%, mixed teams 47%, men-only teams 40%. Women-led ventures often fall further behind post-acceleration. Some gap explained by lower risk tolerance and less openness to outside ownership, but a significant unexplained gap remains likely attributable to gender biases in investment practices.

**Ubicación:** Extracto 4D (p. 14)
**Relevancia:** Benchmark for gender-disaggregated analysis in F-domain and C-domain. The 13-point gap (27% vs 40%) represents the structural gender financing disadvantage in Central America. When ecosystems report low female entrepreneurship, this quantifies the baseline barrier.

### INS-5: Formal vs. informal accelerator-investor relationships
> Two models: (1) Formal — agreement to feed graduates into investment pipeline, often under same umbrella organization; (2) Informal — investors in accelerator's general network, invited to demo days, need-based rather than strategic. Supply-side barriers: ventures too early-stage, entrepreneurs cannot communicate projections, lack documentation, resist ownership sharing. Demand-side: traditional financiers have unrealistic expectations, banks require collateral, no interest rate differentiation for startups. 53% of LatAm impact investors target below-market returns.

**Ubicación:** Extracto 4E (pp. 15-16)
**Relevancia:** Framework for diagnosing why an ecosystem has accelerators but low F-domain scores. The formal/informal distinction helps assess whether accelerator-investor linkages are functional or superficial. The 53% below-market-rate figure identifies impact investors as the natural bridge for early-stage ventures.

### INS-6: Structural barriers in small, volatile economies
> Core challenges: individual Central American markets too small for profitable exits; entrepreneurs cannot articulate cross-border scalability; cultural resistance to equity and foreign investors; size mismatch between impact-profile companies and fund requirements; economic volatility increases risk premiums; banks apply uniform requirements regardless of startup stage. 2016 benchmarks: $69.1B VC in USA vs $3.6B in India vs $621M in Mexico.

**Ubicación:** Extracto 4F (pp. 13, 16)
**Relevancia:** Explains why F-domain scores remain low even when S-domain (accelerators) is moderate. Small market size, volatility, and equity aversion are systemic factors not solvable by accelerator programming alone. Essential context for M3 (sector specialization) and F1 (total investment) scoring.
